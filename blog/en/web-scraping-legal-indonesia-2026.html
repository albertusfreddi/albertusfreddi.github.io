Is Web Scraping Legal in Indonesia? A Developer's Honest Guide (2026)

Albertus Freddi October 7, 2026 10 min read
Read in: EN ID 中文 日本語 한국어

Every week I get at least one inquiry that goes something like: "I need a scraper for [Indonesian website]. Is that legal?" Sometimes they've already been told by someone that scraping is illegal in Indonesia. Sometimes they've been told it's totally fine. Both answers are usually wrong, or at least incomplete.

I'm not a lawyer and this isn't legal advice. But I've built dozens of scraping systems for clients in Indonesia over the past decade, and I've had to learn this space carefully, both to protect myself and to give clients honest assessments of what they're asking for.

Here's how I think about it.

Short version: web scraping in Indonesia is legal in many contexts, but there are specific scenarios, especially involving personal data, circumventing access controls, or collecting data the site owner clearly objects to, where it gets complicated fast.

The Relevant Laws (in Plain Language)

UU PDP, Personal Data Protection Law

Indonesia's Personal Data Protection Law (Undang-Undang Perlindungan Data Pribadi, or UU PDP) came into full effect in 2024. It's the single biggest change to the scraping landscape in recent years.

The key implication: if you scrape personal data (names, email addresses, phone numbers, ID numbers, location data, photos that identify individuals), you're now in UU PDP territory. This means you need a lawful basis for collecting and processing that data, and the person's consent is one of the strongest bases.

Scraping publicly posted personal data (like a business listing with the owner's name and phone) is gray area. Scraping user profiles from a platform where users didn't consent to their data being harvested by third parties is much riskier.

UU ITE, Electronic Information and Transactions Law

Older and broader, UU ITE (as amended in 2023) is the one most people cite when discussing online activity legality. Article 30 is relevant: accessing computer systems "without authorization" or "by violating security measures" is a criminal offense.

In practice, this means: if a website requires a login to access data and you bypass that login to scrape, you're in criminal territory. The law doesn't care that the underlying data might otherwise be public, if you bypassed a security measure to get it, that's illegal access.

Similarly, if a website's Terms of Service explicitly prohibit scraping, and you scrape anyway, you're not automatically committing a crime, ToS violations are civil matters, but you're creating legal exposure that a court could weigh against you.

Copyright Law (UUHC)

Scraped content may be protected by copyright. Copying product descriptions, articles, or other original content and using it commercially without permission is copyright infringement regardless of whether the scraping itself was legal. The act of scraping is separate from what you do with what you scraped.

The Traffic Light Framework

When I assess a scraping project, I put it into one of three buckets:

✓ Low Risk

  • Public price data (no login)
  • Government open data
  • Business contact info publicly listed
  • Your own site / with explicit permission
  • Academic research on public content

⚠ Gray Area

  • Marketplace seller data
  • Social media public posts
  • Aggregating publicly posted personal info
  • Content behind registration (but not paywall)
  • Competitor pricing at scale

✗ High Risk

  • Bypassing login/auth
  • Scraping personal data at scale
  • Violating explicit ToS prohibitions
  • Ignoring robot.txt + rate limits
  • Reselling scraped databases

The robots.txt Question

robots.txt is a convention, not law. Ignoring it isn't inherently illegal under Indonesian law. But it matters for a few reasons:

  • It's evidence of the site owner's intent. If you scraped a site that explicitly disallowed scrapers in its robots.txt, a court will note that you knew the owner objected.
  • Platforms like Tokopedia and Shopee have both robots.txt restrictions and explicit ToS language prohibiting scraping. Ignoring both, at scale, is how you end up with a legal threat letter.
  • Rate limiting is related: hammering a site with hundreds of requests per second can be construed as a denial-of-service attack under UU ITE, regardless of your intent.

My standard practice: always read robots.txt, always rate-limit (1–3 seconds between requests is my baseline), always add a descriptive User-Agent string so the site owner can see what's hitting their server.

The Personal Data Trap

This is where many developers underestimate their exposure. Consider a project I declined last year: a client wanted to scrape all the seller profiles from a major Indonesian marketplace, names, phone numbers, store addresses, to build a B2B contact list for cold outreach.

On the surface, this sounds like scraping public data. The seller profiles are publicly visible, after all. But under UU PDP:

  • Phone numbers and names are personal data
  • The sellers consented to their data being on the marketplace platform, not to being harvested for third-party cold outreach
  • Building a database of this data and using it commercially has no lawful basis under UU PDP

I turned down that project. The client found someone else to do it. That's their choice, but I'd rather not be the developer named in a regulatory complaint.

If your scraping project involves collecting information about identifiable individuals, even publicly posted information, get a lawyer to review it before you build. UU PDP teeth are still being tested in courts, but the risk is real.

What I Tell Clients Before We Start

For every scraping project, I walk clients through three questions:

  1. What data, exactly? Is it prices, product listings, publicly available business info, or is personal data involved? These have different risk profiles.
  2. What's the business use? Internal analytics is different from reselling a data product to third parties.
  3. What does the site owner think? Have they published an API? Does their ToS address scraping? Have they sent previous C&D letters to scrapers? A quick Google search usually surfaces this.

If a project clears all three with confidence, we proceed. If it's gray, I document my assessment, clients acknowledge the risk in writing, and we build in careful technical safeguards. If it's red, I pass.

Technical Safeguards That Reduce Your Legal Exposure

Regardless of the legal analysis, these practices reduce both legal and technical risk:

  • Respect robots.txt, Not because it's legally required, but because it's evidence of good faith
  • Rate limit aggressively, 1–3 second delays, random jitter, off-peak scheduling
  • Identify your bot, Use a User-Agent that includes your contact info
  • Don't store more than you need, If your use case needs current prices, don't also store historical personal data
  • Log what you scraped and when, If you ever need to demonstrate good-faith compliance, logs help
  • Have a takedown process, If a site owner asks you to stop, stop immediately and delete the data

The Indonesian tech and legal landscape around web scraping is still maturing. But the direction is clearly toward more enforcement, not less, especially for personal data. Building good habits now is cheaper than dealing with a regulatory problem later.
